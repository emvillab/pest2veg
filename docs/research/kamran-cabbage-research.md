# Pest2Veg – Cabbage Pest Research

*Research and website content for the cabbage section of Pest2Veg*

## 1. Introduction

My research responsibility for the Pest2Veg group project is cabbage. The website currently includes three cabbage pest entries: mealy cabbage aphid, cabbage caterpillars and cabbage root fly. I researched these pests to make sure the information shown on the website is accurate, relevant to UK growers and supported by reliable sources.

I used sources from the Agriculture and Horticulture Development Board (AHDB) and the Royal Horticultural Society (RHS), together with academic research where it was useful. AHDB was particularly useful because it provides information about pests affecting UK brassica crops. The research focused on how each pest can be recognised, when it is active, the damage it causes, where a grower should look for signs, and suitable ways of managing it. I also considered the limits of using visible symptoms for pest identification because the same symptom can sometimes have more than one cause [1]–[5].

## 2. Mealy Cabbage Aphid

### Identification

The mealy cabbage aphid (*Brevicoryne brassicae*) is a pest of cabbage and other brassica crops. Wingless aphids are green and can grow to about 2.6 mm long, but they are covered in a greyish-white wax that gives them a pale, powdery appearance. Winged aphids have a darker head and body section and can move to new brassica plants [1].

### Life cycle and activity

The aphid can survive the winter on brassica crops and wild plants, mainly as active aphids, although some may survive as eggs. From spring through autumn, winged aphids can move to new crops. AHDB reports that populations can increase quickly during hot, dry weather [1]. This means the number of aphids present can change depending on weather and growing conditions.

### Damage and symptoms

Feeding can first cause small pale areas on leaves. These areas may later become yellow and the leaves can become crumpled or distorted. Young plants can become stunted and, in severe cases, may die. Large infestations can also contaminate the crop with aphids, wax, shed skins and honeydew. The pest can also spread plant viruses, including Turnip mosaic virus and Cauliflower mosaic virus [1].

### Where to look

A grower should look for the greyish-white aphids and colonies on the plant as well as the leaf symptoms. The visible presence of the aphids is useful because yellowing or distorted leaves on their own do not prove that aphids are the cause.

### Prevention and management

AHDB lists several natural enemies of mealy cabbage aphid, including parasitic wasps, ladybirds, hoverflies, lacewings, spiders and insect-killing fungi. Providing habitats that support these natural enemies may help reduce aphid numbers [1]. However, natural enemies may not completely prevent virus transmission because viruses can be spread even when aphid numbers are low [1].

### Research limitation

One limitation is that symptoms such as yellowing, distorted leaves and poor growth can have other causes. Pest2Veg should therefore use these signs to suggest a possible match rather than claiming that the pest has definitely been identified. AHDB also states that there is no established treatment threshold for mealy cabbage aphid on vegetable brassicas [1].

## 3. Cabbage Caterpillars

### Identification

Cabbage caterpillars are not one single species. The term covers the caterpillars of several butterflies and moths that feed on cabbage and other brassicas. Common examples include the large white (*Pieris brassicae*), small white (*Pieris rapae*) and cabbage moth (*Mamestra brassicae*). Other species such as the green-veined white and diamond-back moth can also be found on brassicas [2]. Their appearance varies, so Pest2Veg should not treat all cabbage caterpillars as if they are the same species.

### Life cycle and activity

Adult butterflies and moths lay eggs on brassica leaves. RHS gives May to October as the main activity period for cabbage caterpillars in the UK, although the exact timing varies between species. White butterfly caterpillars are commonly seen in June–July and August–September, while cabbage moth caterpillars are mainly active from July to September [2].

### Damage and symptoms

The main sign is holes eaten in cabbage leaves. Caterpillars and their droppings may also be visible. The type of damage can vary between species. Large white caterpillars mainly feed on the outer leaves, while small white and cabbage moth caterpillars can move into the cabbage heart and damage the inner leaves. Diamond-back moth caterpillars can leave a thin 'window' of leaf tissue after feeding [2].

### Where to look

Both the outer leaves and the inner part of the cabbage should be checked. Growers can look for eggs, caterpillars, fresh feeding holes and droppings. Checking more than one sign is useful because holes in leaves can also be caused by other pests.

### Prevention and management

RHS recommends checking susceptible plants regularly from spring onwards so that a problem can be noticed before large numbers develop [2]. Physical barriers such as fine-mesh crop covers can also be useful for protecting brassica crops from pests. Academic research on integrated pest management supports the wider principle of monitoring crops and combining different control methods rather than relying on a single method [3].

### Research limitation

The main limitation is that 'cabbage caterpillars' is a group containing several species. The website can reasonably suggest 'Cabbage Caterpillars' when the crop and damage match, but the information entered by the user is not detailed enough to identify a particular caterpillar species. This is why the website should describe the result as a possible pest match.

## 4. Cabbage Root Fly

### Identification

Cabbage root fly (*Delia radicum*) is found throughout the UK and attacks cabbage and other brassica crops. Adult flies are greyish-brown and look similar to small houseflies. The larvae cause most of the crop damage. They are pale, legless maggots that grow to around 5–8 mm long [4].

### Life cycle and activity

The pest mainly survives the winter as pupae in the soil. Adults emerge in spring and lay eggs mainly in the soil around the stems of brassica plants. After hatching, the larvae feed mainly on the roots before returning to the soil to pupate. The life cycle is affected by temperature. AHDB reports that there are generally two to three adult generations in southern parts of the UK and around two in northern areas [4].

### Damage and symptoms

Root feeding can cause plants to grow poorly, wilt or die. Newly emerged crops and recently transplanted plants are at greater risk because their root systems are less developed and cannot tolerate as much damage. On larger plants, root damage may not be obvious from the parts of the plant above ground and can sometimes remain unnoticed until harvest [4].

### Where to look

Because the larvae feed mainly below ground, the roots and soil around the base of a poorly growing or wilting cabbage are important areas to inspect. Wilting on its own is not enough to identify cabbage root fly because water stress, disease and other problems can produce similar symptoms.

### Prevention and management

AHDB states that fine-mesh crop covers can help prevent female cabbage root flies from laying eggs on susceptible crops. Monitoring methods include trapping adult flies, checking soil for eggs and using weather-based forecasts to predict egg laying [4]. Academic research also shows that monitoring and forecasting can support decisions about when pest-control measures are most useful, especially as growers increasingly need to combine several methods within integrated pest management [5].

### Research limitation

Cabbage root fly is difficult to identify using only visible symptoms because the damaging larvae are below ground and wilting has many possible causes. Pest2Veg should therefore combine poor growth or wilting with the location of damage around the roots and still present cabbage root fly only as a possible match.

## 5. Conclusion

The research shows that the three cabbage pest entries can be supported by reliable UK information, but it also shows why Pest2Veg should not present its results as definite diagnoses. Mealy cabbage aphid has useful visible signs, but leaf symptoms can have other causes. Cabbage caterpillars include several different species, and cabbage root fly is difficult to identify because most of the damaging activity takes place below ground. For the website, I selected the information that is most useful to a grower: what the pest is, the main damage to look for, where to inspect the plant and basic management information. The full research provides the evidence behind the shorter website content.
